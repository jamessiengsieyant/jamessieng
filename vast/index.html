Days 1–30 · Learn

Learn the machine
before touching it.

  • Read the policyThreshold, asset classes, useful lives, CIP-to-in-service triggers
  • Walk the processRequisition → PO → receipt → bill → payment → asset tag
  • Tie it out by hand, onceFA subledger to GL, myself, so I know where it breaks
  • Sit through closeAnd write down every manual reclass I see
Days 31–60 · Plan

Turn the pattern
into a number.

  • Quantify the reworkHow many reclasses, which vendors, how many hours
  • Find the repeat offendersThe same five judgment calls, every single month
  • Draft the fixWritten policy someone new can apply without asking
  • Get buy-in firstController, procurement, engineering — before I build anything
Days 61–90 · Implement

Ship one thing.
Prove it worked.

  • Pilot narrowOne or two vendor categories. Not the whole ledger.
  • Measure before and afterReclass volume, close timing, post-close adjustments
  • Hand it offDocumented and owned by the team — not by me

A tool only one person can run isn't an improvement.

The idea

Ask the engineer.
Not the accountant.

The person who knows whether a part has a life after this program is the person requisitioning it — on the day they requisition it.

Not accounting, three months later, at close.

The idea · How it works

One required field. That's it.

Engineer opens a requisition
↓ one required question
"Consumed on a flight article, or reusable across programs?"
↓ answer + program tag rides the PO
Consumed → R&D expense, documented at source
Reusable → capex queue with useful life
↓
Lands in NetSuite already classified
The idea · Why it pays

Contemporaneous evidence beats reconstructed memory.

  • Audit-proofIntent documented the day it existed — not reverse-engineered later
  • Capex actuals vs. forecastFalls out for free, by program
  • Fewer reclassesThe judgment happens once, where the answer is obvious
